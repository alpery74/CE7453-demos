Be the loss: arrange five photos and five captions on the circle

A two-tower model puts photos and captions on the same unit circle. The contrastive loss wants each photo next to its own caption and away from the other four. You are the optimizer: drag the ten points, read the loss, and try to beat the number gradient descent reaches.

The loss is the symmetric InfoNCE of slide 6: for each photo a softmax over the five captions at temperature tau, and the same for each caption over the photos. Coinciding pairs are not enough; the pairs must also be spread apart, because every other caption in the batch is a negative. At tau = 0.1 the softmax is sharp and the loss rewards separation; at tau = 0.5 it is soft and plateaus early, which is why CLIP learns its temperature.

Write the query: find the right photos with the fewest words

An archive of twelve site photos sits in a trained joint space. Compose a text query from the word chips; the query embedding is the normalized sum of the word vectors, and the archive is ranked by cosine similarity. Each round names a target; retrieve all three of its photos in the top three with as few words as you can.

Each word has a direction in the shared space, close to the concept it names and, for words like "cracked" or "steel", between two concepts. The query is the mean direction of its words, which is how a text tower treats a phrase to first order. Generic words pull the query toward the middle and cost recall; a specific word is worth more than three vague ones, which is the wording effect on slide 7.

Scores are Gaussian, defects shifted up by d' = 0.7, 1.2 or 2.0 for the weak, typical and strong model. Recall at a budget is the fraction of the twenty defects that land above the cut; it is the number that matters when a person reviews a fixed share of the list, which is slide 10's argument against reporting accuracy for a triage system. Precision in the reviewed set falls as the budget grows while recall rises; the inspector's time sets the trade.

The real system is x(t+1) = A x(t) + B u(t) with a slow damped rotation; the learned model has a perturbed A. Your click sets u toward the point you chose. The planner solves a least-squares problem inside the model for a horizon of twelve steps, as on slide 17; replanning after every real observation is what closes the loop, slide 18. With a large model error the plan-once trajectory ends far from the target while the replanning one still arrives, because each observation corrects the model's drift before it compounds.

Decide when to retrain: three years on call, one button

A deployed model, monitored month by month. The top half of each tile is the input drift index, available at once. The bottom half is the held-out error, which needs labels and arrives three months late. The site starts changing at a month you do not know. Retraining costs 3; every month the true error is out of band costs 2. Press Retrain when you judge it right.

After the change point the input shift grows linearly until the site change is complete; the drift index is its standardized size relative to the data the current model was trained on, and the error grows with the square of that shift. Retraining refits the model to the current inputs, so both restart from zero, and any change still under way shows up again afterwards. The two automatic policies compared at the end are the ones slide 20 contrasts: act on the input monitor, which sees the change at once, or act on the error monitor, which cannot see anything until the labels arrive.

Be the loss: arrange five photos and five captions on the circle

A two-tower model puts photos and captions on the same unit circle. The contrastive loss wants each photo next to its own caption and away from the other four. You are the optimizer: pick the temperature at which a hand layout can reach a loss near zero, then drag the ten points, read the loss, and try to beat the number gradient descent reaches. The loss is the symmetric InfoNCE of slide 6: for each photo a softmax over the five captions at temperature tau, and the same for each caption over the photos. Coinciding pairs are not enough; the pairs must also be spread apart, because every other caption in the batch is a negative. At tau = 0.1 the softmax is sharp and the loss falls close to zero as soon as each photo wins its own caption; at tau = 0.5 the softmax is soft, every other caption keeps weight however far apart the pairs sit, and the best layout on this circle still scores 0.442, which is why CLIP learns its temperature. The floor also depends on the embedding dimension: five pairs placed as a regular simplex in four dimensions score 0.284 at tau = 0.5, below anything reachable on a circle, which is why CLIP embeds on a 512-dimensional sphere.

Write the query: find the right photos with the fewest words

An archive of twelve site photos sits in a trained joint space. Compose a text query from the word chips; the query embedding is the normalized sum of the word vectors, and the archive is ranked by cosine similarity. Each round names a target; retrieve all three of its photos in the top three with as few words as you can. In the fourth round the query is given and plausible, and the task is to name the chip that pulled it away.

Each word has a direction in the shared space, close to the concept it names and, for words like "cracked" or "steel", between two concepts. The query here is the mean direction of its words. A CLIP text tower is a causal transformer read at its end-of-sequence token; its embeddings show bag-of-words behaviour, with word order weakly represented and one off-topic word moving the whole query, and the averaging in this demo stands in for that documented failure mode. A word that names the concept retrieves all three of its photos on its own, while a word that sits in a gap between clusters retrieves a mixture, which is the wording effect on slide 7.

Scores are Gaussian, defects shifted up by d' = 0.7, 1.2 or 2.0 for the weak, middle and strong model. Recall at a budget is the fraction of the twenty defects that land above the cut; it is the number that matters when a person reviews a fixed share of the list, which is slide 10's argument against reporting accuracy for a triage system. Precision in the reviewed set falls as the budget grows while recall rises; the inspector's time sets the trade.

The real system is x(t+1) = A x(t) + B u(t) with a slow damped rotation; the learned model has a perturbed A. Your click sets u toward the point you chose. The replanning planner solves a least-squares problem inside the model over a twelve-step horizon and applies the first input, as on slide 17; the plan-once planner solves one twenty-step problem at the start and runs all twenty inputs open loop. Replanning after every real observation is what closes the loop, slide 18. With a large model error the plan-once trajectory ends far from the target while the replanning one still arrives, because each observation corrects the model's drift before it compounds.

Decide when to retrain: three years on call, one button assignment

A deployed model, monitored month by month. The top half of each tile is the input drift index, available at once. The bottom half is the held-out error, which needs labels and arrives three months late. The site starts changing at a month you do not know. Retraining costs 3; every month the true error is out of band costs 2. The clock pauses after four months for one decision; after that, press Retrain when you judge it right.

After the change point the input shift grows linearly until the site change is complete; the drift index is its standardized size relative to the data the current model was trained on, and the error grows with the square of that shift. Retraining refits the model to the current inputs, so both restart from zero, and any change still under way shows up again afterwards. The two automatic policies compared at the end are the ones slide 20 contrasts: act on the input monitor, which sees the change at once, or act on the error monitor, which cannot see anything until the labels arrive.

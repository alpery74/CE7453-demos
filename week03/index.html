Every cut is one threshold question, and the regions are the leaves; read the finished tree and it is the kind of chart a design code prints, except that the thresholds came from data. The algorithm picks each cut to purify the two sides as much as possible, which is greedy: it never looks two cuts ahead. Each tree is grown to leaves of three points on a resample of the sixty tests, so it follows its own sample's noise; the resamples differ, so the trees disagree, and the disagreement averages away. The shift readout is the root mean square change of the bold curve between two counts; once it drops under 0.3 mm, a fifth of the test noise, more trees change nothing a test could detect. The averaged variance falls to the correlated floor on slide 5, rho times the single-tree variance, which is why random forests also subsample features to lower rho. Beyond 100 kN no tree has data, so every tree returns its last leaf: a bias every transparency shares, and the average inherits it untouched.

Boosting is the surveyor's loop on slide 6: adjust, inspect the residuals, readjust. For squared loss the residual is the negative gradient of the loss, so each stump is a gradient step in function space, and the learning rate is its step size. Small steps need many rounds and overfit slowly; a rate of one fits the training points fast and the held-out curve turns up sooner. The ghost split is the greedy choice that minimizes the residual sum of squares on the residuals you saw. A split at another threshold points in a different direction in function space, and it can put a step where the residuals do not call for one; the learning rate buttons are what set the length of the step.

Permutation importance asks a model fact: how much worse does the fitted model get when it can no longer read this column? Shuffling keeps the column's distribution and destroys its link to the rows, which is why it beats the library default that rewards columns with many cut points, slide 9. Cement and the water-cement ratio say almost the same thing, so with both present the model can lean on either; shuffling one leaves the other to cover for it, and each looks half as important as the signal they share. Remove the twin and the survivor's bar nearly doubles. Slump carries no signal and its bar hovers at zero.

No assign step and no update step can raise the within-cluster scatter, and the assignments can take only finitely many values, so the loop stops, but only at a local answer: start the centroids elsewhere and it can stop somewhere else, which is why slide 11 says to run several starts. The winter cluster here is elongated, because frequency and damping move together as the deck stiffens, and plain distance to a centroid cuts it badly. The mixture on slide 12 gives each cluster a covariance and each day a soft membership; its expectation-maximization loop is the same two steps with ellipses in place of circles, and each step here is one E and one M, so the ellipses grow from the k-means circles while the memberships at the boundaries soften.

The rotating axis: principal components as the direction that holds the most variance

About 150 readings from two strain gauges on the same bridge girder; the gauges respond to the same traffic, so the cloud is tilted. An axis passes through the mean. Drag its end to rotate it and lock in the angle where you think the projections spread the most. Project every centred reading onto a unit vector v and the variance of the projections is v' S v, where S is the covariance of the two channels. S is the normal matrix of Week 1, A'A over n, built from the centred readings in place of the design columns. Rotating the axis sweeps v' S v around a full turn; its maximum over unit vectors is the largest eigenvalue of S, reached when v solves S v = lambda v, and that eigenvector is the first principal component. The second component is the perpendicular direction and holds the remaining variance, so the two fractions add to one. Slide 13.

Only the healthy class exists, so the detector is a model of normal: the mean and covariance of the healthy cloud, slide 14. The Mahalanobis distance measures in the cloud's own units, so one sigma along the long axis and one sigma along the short axis are the same distance; a Euclidean circle treats the short axis as if it were as wide as the long one and goes blind in exactly the direction damage takes. Both thresholds are set for a one percent false alarm rate on the healthy days, and an alarm needs three days in a row over the line, so that a cold day on the long axis does not count as damage. The drift is synthetic and planted along the short axis of the healthy cloud, which is why the Mahalanobis distance wins by about 100 days; with the toggle the same drift runs along the long axis and the two rulers alarm at about the same day.

A hidden layer is a basis the optimizer shapes, slide 17: each unit is sigmoid(k(z - c)), with c the depth where the step sits and k its steepness, the same function as sigmoid(w z + b) with w = k and b = -k c, and the output weights are the heights. Two steps of opposite sign make a bump, and on a bounded interval a sum of enough bumps comes within any tolerance of a continuous profile, which is the mechanism behind the universal approximation theorem on slide 19. The theorem promises that enough units exist; it says nothing about whether gradient descent from a random start will find them, and the fit button sometimes shows that: the same sixteen units land in a different place from a different start, and some starts stall.

The chain rule on a chain: backpropagation through three nodes by hand

One input x passes through a linear node, a tanh node and an output weight, and the loss is the squared error against a target t. The forward values are printed above each node. Type the local derivative at each node, from the loss backwards; once all four are in, their product is dL/dw1, and a finite difference checks it. Then run the backward pass and watch the running product light the nodes from right to left.

Backpropagation is the chain rule organised so that every local derivative is computed once and multiplied into a running product that travels from the loss back to the parameters, slide 20. Each node knows only its own input and output, so its local derivative needs nothing from the rest of the network: 2(y - t) at the loss, w2 at the output weight, 1 - h^2 at the tanh, and x at the first weight. The running product arriving at a node is the gradient of the loss with respect to that node's output; multiplying by the local derivative passes it one node further back. The finite difference, (L(w1 + e) - L(w1 - e)) / 2e, is the check every implementation should pass.

Every cut is one threshold question, and the regions are the leaves; read the finished tree and it is the kind of chart a design code prints, except that the thresholds came from data. The algorithm picks each cut to purify the two sides as much as possible, which is greedy: it never looks two cuts ahead. The nudge resamples the ninety cases with replacement and refits; cuts jump and sometimes whole branches swap, which is the instability slide 4 names and the variance that averaging will fix on slide 5.

Each tree is grown to leaves of three points on a resample of the sixty tests, so it follows its own sample's noise; the resamples differ, so the trees disagree, and the disagreement averages away. The averaged variance falls to the correlated floor on slide 5, rho times the single-tree variance, which is why random forests also subsample features to lower rho. Beyond 100 kN no tree has data, so every tree returns its last leaf: a bias every transparency shares, and the average inherits it untouched.

Readjust on the residuals: be the boosting loop, one stump at a time

Mid-span deflection of a steel girder against deck temperature, a nonlinear record. The model starts as the plain mean. Below it are the residuals. Click a temperature on the residual strip to split there: the next stump takes the mean residual on each side, shrunk by the learning rate, and is added to the model. The algorithm's own best split is shown as a ghost after each of your choices. Keep going and watch the two error curves.

Boosting is the surveyor's loop on slide 6: adjust, inspect the residuals, readjust. For squared loss the residual is the negative gradient of the loss, so each stump is a gradient step in function space, and the learning rate is its step size. Small steps need many rounds and overfit slowly; a rate of one fits the training points fast and the held-out curve turns up sooner. The ghost split is the greedy choice that minimizes the residual sum of squares; yours need not match it, and a worse split is simply a smaller step.

Shuffle a column: permutation importance, and two columns that carry one signal

Two hundred concrete mixes with their 28-day strength, and a nearest-neighbour model fitted to five columns. Click a column header to shuffle that column into noise on the held-out rows and read how far the error climbs; the bar underneath is that column's importance. Two columns, cement content and the water-cement ratio, carry nearly the same information. Remove one and watch the other's bar.

Permutation importance asks a model fact: how much worse does the fitted model get when it can no longer read this column? Shuffling keeps the column's distribution and destroys its link to the rows, which is why it beats the library default that rewards columns with many cut points, slide 9. Cement and the water-cement ratio say almost the same thing, so with both present the model can lean on either; shuffling one leaves the other to cover for it, and each looks half as important as the signal they share. Remove the twin and the survivor's bar nearly doubles. Slump carries no signal and its bar hovers at zero.

Assign, update, repeat: run k-means by hand on a year of bridge vibration data

Each point is one day: the first natural frequency across, the damping ratio up, from a monitored bridge whose stiffness changes with the seasons. Click on the plane to place centroids, up to four. Then press assign to colour every day by its nearest centroid, and update to move each centroid to the mean of its days. Repeat until nothing moves, read the scatter, and try a different starting placement. Then let a Gaussian mixture give the clusters shape.

Every assign step and every update step lowers the within-cluster scatter, and the assignments can take only finitely many values, so the loop stops, but only at a local answer: start the centroids elsewhere and it can stop somewhere else, which is why slide 11 says to run several starts. The winter cluster here is elongated, because frequency and damping move together as the deck stiffens, and plain distance to a centroid cuts it badly. The mixture on slide 12 gives each cluster a covariance and each day a soft membership; its expectation-maximization loop is the same two steps with ellipses in place of circles.

The thin side of the ellipse: which ruler notices the damage first?

Two natural frequencies of a healthy bridge over 150 days make the cloud; they move together with temperature, so the cloud is a tilted ellipse, and its principal axes are the eigenvectors of the covariance. Drag today's reading anywhere and read its distance by the two rulers. Then drag the day marker along the monitoring record below: damage starts on an unknown day and drifts the reading out through the thin side.

Only the healthy class exists, so the detector is a model of normal: the mean and covariance of the healthy cloud, slide 14. The Mahalanobis distance measures in the cloud's own units, so one sigma along the long axis and one sigma along the short axis are the same distance; a Euclidean circle treats the short axis as if it were as wide as the long one and goes blind in exactly the direction damage takes. Both thresholds are set for a one percent false alarm rate on the healthy days, and an alarm needs three days in a row over the line, so that a cold day on the long axis does not count as damage; the delay between onset and the alarm is set by the drift rate against the feature noise.

Steps into bumps: build a soil profile out of hidden units, then let the optimizer try

The grey curve is a shear strength profile with depth. Each hidden unit is one soft step, a sigmoid placed at a depth with a height and a steepness. Click on the plot to add a unit at that depth; drag a unit's knob on the lane below to set where it steps (across) and how high (up); drag its tail to set the steepness. The blue curve is the sum. Get the error down by hand, then let gradient descent place sixteen units.

A hidden layer is a basis the optimizer shapes, slide 17: each unit is sigmoid(w x + b), with b placing the step and w setting its steepness, and the output weights are the heights. Two steps of opposite sign make a bump, bumps add up to anything, which is the mechanism behind the universal approximation theorem on slide 19. The theorem promises that enough units exist; it says nothing about whether gradient descent from a random start will find them, and the fit button sometimes shows that: the same sixteen units land in a different place from a different start, and some starts stall.

The ground is a Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel (lengthscale 15 m, prior standard deviation 15 kPa) and every reading carries sensor noise with standard deviation 5 kPa. The blue zone is the 90 percent band on the true strength: epistemic, and it narrows wherever you drill. The grey zone is the band on what the next borehole would read: it adds the noise back and can never fall below plus or minus 8 kPa. Drilling the same spot twice shows the difference most plainly. The two readings disagree, the blue band at that spot tightens, and the grey band stays. Slide 4: more boreholes shrink the second tank and never the first.

Your sketch is resampled at forty depths and scored under five squared-exponential kernels with lengthscales 0.4, 1, 2.5, 6 and 15 m. The score is the log marginal likelihood, the probability the kernel assigns to the whole curve at once, with a small noise term so that a hand tremor is not fatal. A kernel with a short lengthscale can explain a jagged curve but pays for it by spreading its probability over far more curves; a long lengthscale concentrates its probability on smooth curves and is caught out by a single kink. That trade is slide 8 in one number, and it is what fitting a variogram does by eye.

Each network has one hidden layer of twelve tanh units and is fitted to the eight readings by a few hundred Adam steps. The deep ensemble trains five of them from different seeds and reports their mean plus or minus 1.645 standard deviations; the members agree near the holes and drift apart in the gaps, so the band widens there for free. Monte Carlo dropout trains one network with twenty percent of its hidden units switched off at random, keeps the switching on at prediction time, and reads the spread of twenty forward passes. The Gaussian process band is the one from the drill tab, and in the gaps it returns to the prior width of plus or minus 25 kPa. Both network approximations are cheap and neither is calibrated by construction: the share of the truth each band actually covers is on the right, and it is set by architecture and training length as much as by the data. Slide 9. That gap between a band and a promise is what the next tabs close.

Weigh the neighbors: kriging weights on a plan of the site

Seven boreholes are plotted on a plan of a 200 by 100 m site with their strengths. Drag the crosshair to where you need an estimate: each borehole grows or shrinks with the weight it gets, and the ring around the crosshair is the kriging standard deviation. Click an empty spot to mark where you would add a borehole, lock it in, and the page scores your spot against a grid of alternatives. Three things to find are listed on the right.

The weights are simple kriging weights, w = K^-1 k*, with a squared-exponential kernel of lengthscale 30 m and prior standard deviation 12 kPa about a known mean of 50 kPa, plus a 1 kPa nugget. They depend only on where the boreholes are, never on what they read, which is why moving the crosshair redraws them and adding a borehole redraws everyone else's. A borehole hidden behind another, as seen from the crosshair, can receive a negative weight: that is the screening effect, and it is the point where kriging stops being a weighted average in the everyday sense. Two boreholes close together share the weight one of them would have had. Far from every borehole the weights fade, the estimate returns to the mean, and the ring grows to the prior.

The model's own band is a Gaussian with the noise level it fitted, and it fitted too small a noise, so its nominal 90 percent catches about three quarters of the truth. Nothing in the fit warns you; only counting on held-out cases does. The conformal wrap, slide 11, is the staircase: sixty calibration residuals in absolute value, sorted. Cutting at rank k promises coverage k over 61 and charges the k-th residual as the half width, so the staircase is also a width-against-coverage curve, and its last few steps are the expensive ones. The promise holds for any model, with no distributional assumption, as long as the test cases are exchangeable with the calibration cases. The next tab breaks that assumption on purpose.

The curve on the right is the width-against-coverage curve of slide 12: the sorted clay calibration residuals read as the half width that buys each level of coverage. The orange line is the width in use, and the two dots on it are the coverage that width actually achieves on the clay cards and on the sand cards. On clay the dot tracks the promise up to sampling noise. Dragging the line wider buys sand coverage at a price in width that the clay calibration never quoted. Week 2's remedy applies: calibrate on data drawn the way the deployment will be, or keep one calibration set per region.

Both decoders share one smooth map from the two coordinates to a profile with two bumps whose positions and heights follow the coordinates. The autoencoder's objective pins its decoder only at the forty training points; between them nothing constrains it, so this page models that freedom honestly as a random function conditioned to vanish at the training points, which is a Gaussian process posterior from the morning's lecture with the training embeddings as data. Where that function is large the decoded profile is rough, and a roughness score above 3 counts as a hole. The variational autoencoder's prior term pulls every embedding toward the origin and gives each a spread, so the training blobs overlap and cover the disc of the prior with no gap to fall into. Slide 20's advice stands: decode a grid and look before you trust it.

Pack the blobs: be the encoder and feel the two terms pull

Six training fields, six blobs on the latent plane. Drag a blob by its center to move it, drag its rim to change its spread. A balance on the right weighs the reconstruction term against the prior term. Three rounds change what the balance counts: both terms (the opening round), then the prior term weighted eight times, then reconstruction alone. In each round pack the blobs for the lowest total, let the optimizer finish the job, then draw samples from the prior and see what they decode to.

Each blob is an encoder output q(z given x): a mean and an isotropic spread. The decoder here is fixed and simple: a point z decodes to the training fields mixed by how strongly each blob claims it, so blobs that overlap decode to a blur of their fields and the reconstruction term rises. The prior term is the closed-form KL divergence of each blob from the unit Gaussian: it charges distance from the origin and any spread other than one. With reconstruction alone the best packing is tiny blobs far apart, and the prior samples land in the gaps and decode to blurs of unrelated fields: slide 15's holes. With both terms the blobs crowd the unit disc and overlap a little, and most prior samples decode to something field-like. With the prior term weighted eight times, the cheapest packing puts every blob on the origin with spread one, the decoder can no longer tell the fields apart, and every sample is the training mean: slide 21's posterior collapse, found by hand.

Route the gradient: get the signal back through a random draw

The loss sits at the right end of the circuit and the encoder's weights at the left. Press Send to launch a gradient pulse backwards. It dies at the sampling node, because a draw from a distribution has no derivative with respect to that distribution's parameters. Drag the dice off the main line onto the side rail and send the pulse again.

In the first wiring the sampler takes mu and sigma and emits z by rolling. The pulse arrives at z and finds no path to mu or sigma: a random draw is not a function of its parameters in the sense a derivative needs. The rewired circuit rolls the dice first, on a rail that no gradient has to cross, and then computes z = mu + sigma times eps with plain arithmetic. Now z is a deterministic function of mu and sigma for the eps that was drawn, the derivatives are 1 and eps, and the pulse runs through to the encoder. Slide 19: one line of algebra, and the reason the model trains by ordinary gradient descent. The score-function estimator would also get a gradient through, with no rewiring, but its variance is far too large for a decoder of this size.

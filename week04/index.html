Twelve stations: carry a signal forward and a gradient back without losing either

A twelve-layer network drawn as a leveling run: the signal passes station to station on the way out, the gradient passes back on the way home. Each gauge is the spread of what arrives at that station, on a log scale, with the green band marking spreads that survive. Choose the activation and set the initial weight gain until all twenty-four gauges stay in the band. Then see what a skip connection does to a run that has already failed.

Each station multiplies the spread by a factor and depth compounds it, the same bookkeeping as error propagation along a traverse. A sigmoid's derivative peaks at a quarter, so the gradient loses most of its spread at every station on the way back, slide 5; no gain fixes that, because a large gain saturates the units and the derivative goes to zero anyway. With ReLU, half the units are off, so a weight variance of 1/n halves the signal variance at every station and the spread arriving falls by the square root of two; He's 2/n restores both, slide 6. At gain zero every unit in a station computes the same thing and the gradients are identical, the symmetry that never breaks. A skip connection adds the station's input to its output, so the gradient has an identity path home whatever the activation does, slide 17.

This relay cannot show an exploding gradient with tanh or sigmoid: a large gain drives the state into saturation, where the derivative is near zero, so the spread falls instead of growing. The linear mode of the Week 5 reach tab shows the exploding case: ../week05/#reach.

Slide 17 names the residual block. The skip button here is the residual connection; a full block is a convolution, a normalization layer, an activation and a second convolution, with the block's input added to its output. The addition is what gives the gradient its identity path, and stacking such blocks is how networks of a hundred layers train at all.

The time trial: three optimizers, one badly scaled valley, a stopwatch

Fit pile settlement, in millimetres, to two columns: the test load in meganewtons, which runs from 0.1 to 0.9, and the months since installation, which run from 1 to 60. In raw units the months column is seventy times wider than the load column, so the loss is a long thin valley. Three runners start from the same point: plain gradient descent, momentum, and Adam. Set one learning rate for all three, press start, and watch each lane's distance to the bottom shrink. Then standardize the columns and run the same race again.

Plain descent must use a step small enough for the steep direction, then crawls along the flat one; the ratio of the two curvatures, the condition number, says how many times slower the crawl is, the Week 1 story again. Momentum averages recent gradients, so the zigzag across the valley cancels and the crawl along it accumulates, slide 8. Adam divides each coordinate's step by the running root-mean-square of its own gradient, which rescales the valley to nearly round on its own; that statistic is not curvature, and it is why weight decay has to be decoupled in AdamW. Standardizing the columns rounds the valley for everyone, which is what normalization layers do between layers, slide 7, and after it plain descent with a large step is hard to beat.

Slide 11 gives the four shapes. Both curves stalled high and close together is underfitting, and the cure depends on what the run ran out of: capacity when the curves have gone flat, epochs when they were still falling at the last one on the axis. Training still falling while validation turns back up is overfitting: decay, dropout, an earlier stop, or more data. A loss that thrashes or climbs is a step too large; a creep that continues to the end of a long run is a step too small, and there the cure is a larger step. Early stopping, slide 10, keeps the weights from the epoch where validation was lowest, and because that epoch was chosen by looking at the validation curve, the validation number at that epoch is no longer an honest estimate; the third split is. Weight decay and early stopping fail differently, so the clinic lets you combine them by applying one prescription after another.

A convolutional layer is the sliding sum of products you used for blurring and edge finding, with the nine entries made trainable, slide 14. The learned kernel is whatever nine numbers make the response match the traced map best, and on a crack patch it usually comes out as a second-derivative detector, a Laplacian with its own weights, because a crack is a dark line thinner than the pavement texture around it. Nobody told it about the handbook. The score is the separation between the mean response on crack pixels and on pavement, in units of the pavement's spread. A dense layer that maps the same patch to the same map would hold one weight per pair of pixels, the number on the last line, and would have to learn the crack separately at every position, slide 15.

Stack for reach: build a layer stack whose top unit can see the whole spall

Each layer adds its kernel size minus one, times the product of every stride below it, slide 16. Three by three kernels add two pixels of reach each and cost nine weights per channel pair; a stride of two or a pooling layer doubles the increment of everything above it for nothing, which is why the field grows geometrically and the map shrinks. Seven by seven kernels reach fast but cost five times the weights of three by three, and two stacked three by threes reach as far as one five by five with fewer weights and a nonlinearity between them. A unit whose field never reaches across the spall cannot tell a spall from a stain, however many channels it has. The two build rules carry the rest of the argument: a pool on the raw image is the cheapest way to reach further and it discards the two-pixel crack before any filter has looked at it, and a total stride past 8 leaves a map too coarse to say where on the deck the spall is. Reach obtained by discarding resolution is reach a segmentation head cannot use.

Fifty labels: the learning curve from scratch against the curve on a borrowed trunk

A defect classifier is trained with n labelled patches, for n from five to two thousand, two ways: from scratch, and as a small head on a pretrained trunk whose features were learned on someone else's images. The dial sets the domain gap, how far the trunk's features are rotated away from the ones this task needs. Drag the dial and read where the two curves cross; a self-supervised trunk pretrained on your own unlabelled archive sits at zero gap by construction.

The trunk carries the features and the head is a handful of weights, so with fifty labels the head is well determined and the trunk's features do the work; from scratch, the same fifty labels have to pin down every weight, slide 20. The gap is the caution on the same slide: rotate the features away from what the task needs and the borrowed trunk plateaus below the scratch curve, however many labels you add, because a frozen trunk cannot learn what it was never shown. Fine-tuning at a small rate moves the trunk toward the task and closes part of the gap; self-supervision on your own archive removes it, slide 22, by pretraining where the images are plentiful and the labels absent.

Disclosure: the 95 against 77 percent at fifty labels is a designed outcome. The supervised trunk's features are the true features of this task rotated by the gap angle, so at zero gap the head has only five weights to fit. Real trunks are never this well matched, and the toy's numbers are the slide's numbers by construction. The trunk-origin buttons give the other two cases of slide 22: a self-supervised trunk, the same features carrying twenty percent extra noise because no label guided them, and a random trunk, four random projections that no pretraining shaped.

Flips and rotations multiply fifty crack patches into four hundred at no labelling cost because a crack is still a crack upside down, slide 21. The same flip on a slope aspect map manufactures a wrong label at every rotated copy, and a model trained on it learns that aspect does not matter, which is false. Brightness jitter is harmless for a photograph and destroys a calibrated radiometric band. Every row of the board is a physical statement about what the label depends on; the experiment shows the two ways a wrong stamp costs you, a model that cannot tell the classes apart when the label depends on orientation, and a model that never learned the invariance it should have when a harmless augmentation was refused.

The loss is the fitted law L(N, D) = E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta of Hoffmann et al. (2022). The constants are those of Besiroglu et al. (2024), the Chinchilla replication that refitted the published data: E = 1.82, A = 482, B = 2085, alpha = 0.348, beta = 0.366. Along one budget the loss has an interior minimum, and at that minimum the two correction terms are of comparable size, which is why the optimal tokens per parameter sit near twenty rather than near one or one thousand.

Per-token accuracy follows p(N) = 0.05 + 0.95 / (1 + exp(-1.5 (log10 N - 9))), a smooth curve with nothing emergent in it. Exact match over L tokens is p^L, which stays near zero until p is close to one and then rises steeply. Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) showed that most reported emergent abilities behave this way under a continuous metric, which is the point of slide 9.

The tokeniser on this page is a fixed rule: split on spaces and punctuation, cut words longer than six letters into pieces of four, and cut numbers into groups of three digits. Real tokenisers use byte-pair encoding (Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, 2016), which learns its pieces from data: frequent strings become single tokens and rare strings are split into many. Numbers and units tokenise badly under either rule. A height such as 102.3481 m becomes five or more tokens whose boundaries carry no arithmetic meaning, which is one reason models handle survey figures worse than survey prose, slide 10. The context window is the fixed number of tokens a model reads at once; text beyond it is absent from the prompt, and every token inside it costs money and attention.

The model here is Bayesian linear regression with a Gaussian prior on (a, b) standing in for pretraining. Each example in the prompt conditions the posterior in closed form; the completion is the posterior predictive mean. Nothing is gradient-updated, which is slide 11's claim that in-context learning is inference. Two well-spread examples pin the slope; four clustered ones do not, which is slide 12's point about what makes a good prompt.

Each task's true update is built from singular values that decay geometrically, fast for a narrow task and slowly for a broad one. The rank-r adapter keeps the first r directions, so the relative error is the square root of the energy left in the tail, which is what the readout and the residual panel report. The adapter here is the leading r terms of the true update's singular value decomposition, the best rank-r approximation that exists by the Eckart-Young theorem, so every error on this bench is a floor for a trained LoRA adapter: real training starts from B = 0 and fits the adapter to task data alone, and the rank is settled on a validation set. LoRA's premise on slide 16 is that real fine-tuning updates have the fast-decaying kind of spectrum; the bench shows what happens when they do not.

Half the items are easy (true probability of a right answer 0.88 to 0.99) and half are hard (0.35 to 0.75); the calibrated model states that probability, the overconfident one states 0.6 + 0.4 p^0.6 plus noise, so its confidences are both inflated and jumbled. The interval is Wilson's, slide 19. The threshold is chosen on the first two hundred items and the bound is then re-estimated at that threshold on a second two hundred drawn the same way: the largest of about two hundred Wilson bounds computed on one sample covers the truth less often than 95 percent of the time, so a figure quoted after a threshold search holds only on data that took no part in the search. Refusing low-confidence items buys accuracy only as far as the stated confidence actually ranks the items, which is the practical content of slide 20.

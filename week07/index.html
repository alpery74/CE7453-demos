Extend the line: predict the big run from four small ones

Four models have been trained, from one million to one hundred million parameters, and their losses are plotted on log axes. Lay the ruler through them by dragging its two handles, read off your prediction for a ten billion parameter run, then pay for the run and see where it lands.

The runs follow L = E + A / N^alpha with E = 1.8, the entropy floor of the data. In the first exercise the vertical axis is the reducible loss L - E, so the law is a straight line on log axes and the ruler extrapolates two decades without error. In the second the raw loss is plotted, the floor bends the points, and a ruler laid through the measured range predicts a loss below the floor. That is slide 5's narrow reading: the straight line is a statement about the range you measured, and about the quantity you plotted.

Fit the run: stack the memory of one training step onto the card

A training step holds the weights, their gradients, the optimizer's two moments and the activations of the batch in flight. Everything here is set by touching the picture: click a model crate to load it, click above the stack to add a sample of activations and click a green brick to drop one, click the weights brick to switch mixed precision, drag the Adam brick onto a second device to shard the states, click the ghost column to add a device, click the loop to accumulate, and drag the card's top edge to change the card. Make the run fit with an effective batch of 256.

Per parameter, a plain fp32 step keeps 4 bytes of weight, 4 of gradient and 8 of Adam moments: 16 bytes, so a 1.3 B model is 21 GB before a single activation. Mixed precision moves the arithmetic to 2 bytes but keeps the 4-byte master copy and the 8-byte moments, so it saves activation memory and time, little else, slide 7. Gradient accumulation buys batch size with wall-clock time and no memory. Data parallel multiplies the batch, and only sharding the states (ZeRO, FSDP) divides the per-device memory. The target here is an effective batch of 256 on the card you have.

Cut the image into tokens, then take the positions away

A 64 by 64 aerial scene becomes a sequence by cutting it into patches. Drag the corner handle of the first patch to set the patch size, click a patch to see the vector that becomes its token and the position tag that goes with it, then shuffle the sequence and look at what a model without position tags would be reasoning about.

A patch of p by p pixels in three bands is flattened to 3 p squared numbers and projected to the model width; that vector plus a position tag is the token, slide 8. Nothing in the attention block knows the patches came from a grid: the shuffled image is the model's view without the tags. Smaller patches give a longer sequence and a finer grid, at quadratic cost in the score matrix; larger patches are cheap and coarse. The convolution of Week 4 had the grid built in, which is why it still wins on small labelled sets.

The reach test: compose a sparse pattern that still reaches everything

Forty-eight positions in a record. Full attention lets each one read all 48 at once. Compose a cheaper pattern on the stencil row: drag the edge of the orange window to widen it, drag the second tooth of the comb to set a stride, and click the slots above the first positions to make them global tokens. Click a position on the record to make it the source and watch how many hops its information needs to reach everyone. The challenge: everything within two hops, at most twelve entries per row.

-

-

-

A pattern is a fixed set of pairs that will be scored, chosen before training, slide 10. A window alone needs T / w hops to cross the record, which is the long reach attention was built for, thrown away. A stride adds a coarse grid, so distant positions meet in two hops. A global token is a shared summary that everyone reads and writes, and it collapses the diameter to two hops at a cost of one entry per row. The pattern is a prior about which pairs matter; the record's physics, not a paper about text, should pick it.

The step sequencer: tap an input into a state space layer and watch what it keeps

A two-state linear system, a damped oscillator, discretized with a zero order hold. Click steps on the input row to place impulses. The state evolves one step at a time and the output traces below; the kernel row shows the same layer as a convolution. The system itself is set on the pole map at the right: drag the pole left for faster decay, up for faster rotation. The step size is the spacing of the sample ticks on the time ruler: drag a tick. Set the layer to remember a cue 24 steps back.

The continuous system is x' = A x + B u with A = [[-lambda, omega], [-omega, -lambda]], whose eigenvalues are the pole pair -lambda plus or minus i omega on the map, and y = C x. Holding u constant over a step of length Delta gives the recurrence x_k = A_bar x_(k-1) + B_bar u_k with A_bar = exp(A Delta), slide 15, the Kalman prediction step with learned matrices, slide 16. Unrolled, y is the convolution of u with the kernel K_k = C A_bar^k B_bar, slide 19. The half-life in steps is ln 2 / (lambda Delta): the pole and the step size set the timescale together, and a learned layer sets both from the data. The pole map is the control engineer's picture of the same object.

The distractor test: a fixed layer against a selective one

A record holds one cue early on and a burst of distractors somewhere after it. Two state space layers read the record: one with fixed matrices, one whose step size depends on the input. Drag the burst along the record by its body and change its length by dragging its right edge. The fixed layer's step size is the height of its gate bars: drag them up or down. Then read how much of the cue each layer still holds at the end.

Both layers run h_k = a_k h_(k-1) + (1 - a_k) u_k with a_k = exp(-Delta_k). The fixed layer uses one Delta for every step, so every distractor both decays the cue and writes itself in. The selective layer, slide 18, computes Delta from the input: large for a step it recognizes as a cue, near zero for a step it treats as noise, so the state holds through the burst. That is the gate of Week 5 in a cleaner form, and the reason the layer no longer has a single convolutional kernel: the kernel now depends on the record.

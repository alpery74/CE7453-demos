Extend the line: predict the big run from four small ones

Four models have been trained, from one million to one hundred million parameters, and their reducible losses are plotted on log axes with the floor subtracted. Lay the ruler through them by dragging its two handles, commit to whether the ten billion parameter run will land on your line, then pay for the run and see where it lands.

The runs follow L = E + A / N^alpha with E = 1.8, the entropy floor of the data, and each measured loss carries noise of a few percent from its finite validation set. With the floor subtracted the vertical axis is the reducible loss L - E, where the law is a straight line on log axes. A ruler laid through the middle of the four cheap runs predicts the big run to within a few percent of its reducible loss, and the gate scores that prediction against a stated tolerance of ten percent, drawn as the orange band at the big size. Four runs over two decades fix the exponent to a few percent, two more decades of extrapolation multiply that error, and the run itself settles what the ruler claimed. All four runs here hold the token budget fixed, so N is the only budget on the axis, which is the parameter-only picture of Kaplan et al. (2020). Hoffmann et al. (2022) fit the compute-optimal law L(N, D) = E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta, in which parameters and tokens grow together under a fixed compute budget, about twenty tokens per parameter. Four small runs set the exponent and the exponent predicts the loss of a run a hundred times larger, slide 5. The next tab plots the same runs without subtracting the floor.

With the floor E = 1.8 left inside the loss, the points bend as they approach it, and a ruler laid through the measured range runs under the curve. This is slide 5's narrow reading of a scaling law: the straight line is a statement about the range that was measured and about the quantity that was plotted. The law is a power law in the reducible loss.

Fit the run: stack the memory of one training step onto the card

A training step holds the weights, their gradients, the optimizer's two moments and the activations of the batch in flight. Everything here is set by touching the picture: click a model crate to load it, click above the stack to add a sample of activations and click a green brick to drop one, click the weights brick to switch mixed precision, drag the Adam brick onto a second device to shard the states, click the ghost column to add a device, click the loop to accumulate, and drag the card's top edge to change the card. Commit to a number of cards first; the bench unlocks after that.

Per parameter, a plain fp32 step keeps 4 bytes of weight, 4 of gradient and 8 of Adam moments: 16 bytes, so a 1.3 B model is 19.4 GB before a single activation. Mixed precision moves the arithmetic to 2 bytes but keeps the 4-byte master copy and the 8-byte moments, so it saves activation memory and time, little else, slide 7. Gradient accumulation buys batch size with wall-clock time and no memory. Data parallel multiplies the batch, and only sharding the states (ZeRO, FSDP) divides the per-device memory. The 7 B model carries 104 GB of weights, master copy, gradients and moments before any activation, so on 24 GB cards the static stack has to be sharded over eight devices; accumulation then buys the rest of the batch.

A patch of p by p pixels in three bands is flattened to 3 p squared numbers and projected to the model width; that vector plus a position tag is the token, slide 8. Nothing in the attention block knows the patches came from a grid: the shuffled image is the model's view without the tags. Smaller patches give a longer sequence and a finer grid, at quadratic cost in the score matrix; larger patches are cheap and coarse. The convolution of Week 4 had the grid built in, which is why it still wins on small labelled sets.

The reach test: compose a sparse pattern that still reaches everything

Twenty-four positions in a record. Full attention lets each one read all 24 at once. Compose a cheaper pattern on the stencil row: drag the edge of the orange window to widen it, drag the second tooth of the comb to set a stride, and click the slots above the first positions to make them global tokens. Click a position on the record to make it the source and watch how many hops its information needs to reach everyone. Pick the device you expect to win before the stencil unlocks.

A pattern is a fixed set of pairs that will be scored, chosen before training, slide 10. A window alone needs T / w hops to cross the record, which is the long reach attention was built for, thrown away. A stride adds a coarse grid, so distant positions meet in two hops. A global token is a shared summary that everyone reads and writes, and it collapses the diameter to two hops at a cost of one entry per ordinary row; the global row itself reads all T. The pattern is a prior about which pairs matter, and the physics of the record should pick it.

The step sequencer: tap an input into a state space layer and watch what it keeps

A two-state linear system, a damped oscillator, discretized with a zero order hold. Click steps on the input row to place impulses. The state evolves one step at a time and the output traces below; the kernel row shows the same layer as a convolution. The system itself is set on the pole map at the right: first click where the pole belongs for a half-life of 12 steps, then drag it left for faster decay, up for faster rotation. The step size is the spacing of the sample ticks on the time ruler: drag a tick. The output can be computed by the recurrence or by the convolution; both are shown to agree.

The continuous system is x' = A x + B u with A = [[-lambda, omega], [-omega, -lambda]], whose eigenvalues are the pole pair -lambda plus or minus i omega on the map, and y = C x. Holding u constant over a step of length Delta gives the recurrence x_k = A_bar x_(k-1) + B_bar u_k with A_bar = exp(A Delta), slide 15, the Kalman prediction step with learned matrices, slide 16. Unrolled, y is the convolution of u with the kernel K_k = C A_bar^k B_bar, slide 19; the toggle computes the output both ways and the largest difference between them is rounding error. The half-life in steps is ln 2 / (lambda Delta): the pole and the step size set the timescale together, and a learned layer sets both from the data. The pole map is the control engineer's picture of the same object.

The distractor test: a fixed layer against a selective one

A record holds one cue early on and a burst of distractors somewhere after it. Three state space layers read the record: one with fixed matrices, one whose gate you draw by hand, and one whose step size depends on the input. Drag the burst along the record by its body and change its length by dragging its right edge. The fixed layer's step size is the height of its gate bars: drag them up or down. Paint your own gate on the middle row, lock it in, and compare how much of the cue each layer still holds at the end.

All three layers run h_k = a_k h_(k-1) + (1 - a_k) u_k with a_k = exp(-Delta_k). The fixed layer uses one Delta for every step, so every distractor both decays the cue and writes itself in. A hand-drawn gate shows what a good Delta_k looks like: open at the cue, shut everywhere else. The cue stands at u = 2.4 and the distractors fall between 0.6 and 1.4, so the height of a step is enough to tell them apart. The selective layer, slide 18, computes its Delta from that height alone, Delta_k = 1.5 sigmoid(12 (u_k - 1.8)), which opens to 1.50 at the cue and stays under 0.02 through the burst, so the state holds. That is the gate of Week 5 in a cleaner form, and the reason the layer no longer has a single convolutional kernel: the kernel now depends on the record.

The weights are softmax(s) and the output is the sum of w_i v_i, the two lines on slide 5. Because the weights are positive and sum to one, every output lies inside the convex hull of the values: attention can only interpolate what the encoder offered. In query mode the scores are q dot k_i over the square root of d, with the keys set equal to the values for display, and dragging the query is what a trained decoder does when it changes what it needs, slide 7.

The saturation bench: why the square root of d is in the formula

A dot product of d independent unit-variance terms has variance d, so the raw scores spread by about sqrt(d): 2 at d = 4, 8 at d = 64, 23 at d = 512. The softmax of scores that far apart is a one-hot vector, and the derivative of a one-hot softmax is zero, so the layer cannot learn. Dividing by sqrt(d) puts the scores back on a scale of one at every width, which is the justification on slide 8. The bar under each fan is the Frobenius norm of the softmax Jacobian, the factor that multiplies every gradient passing through the layer; the fan of lines is the head view used by attention visualizers such as BertViz.

Four relations that real heads learn on their own, slide 14: a local head that reads the neighbouring steps, an anchor head that keeps returning to one position such as the start of the record, an offset head that reads a fixed number of steps back, and a periodic head that reads the same phase of a cycle. The maps are a record of where the weight went, slide 10; whether the attended position mattered is a separate question that the value vectors decide.

Self attention is permutation equivariant: permute the inputs and the outputs are permuted the same way, with no other change, which is the whole of slide 15's problem. The sinusoidal code gives every position a vector of sines and cosines at wavelengths from a few steps to thousands, drawn here as a barcode; click a column and the others are tinted by their similarity to it, which falls off with distance in both directions. A learned table does the same job inside its length and has nothing to say beyond it.

Build the mask: let each position read as much as it honestly can

A decoder learns to predict the next value at every position of a six-step record, all positions at once. The grid says which key positions (columns) each query position (rows) may read. Click cells to allow or block them, then train, and read which rows learned and which cheated.

Position t is asked for the value at t + 1. If its row may read any column at or beyond t + 1, the model can copy the answer, the training loss there falls to nothing, and nothing useful is learned: leakage by architecture, slide 17. If the row may read nothing, the position predicts from nothing. The mask that is honest everywhere and wastes nothing is the lower triangle including the diagonal, implemented by setting the blocked scores to minus infinity before the softmax. Padding gets the same treatment in the columns.

The bill: what a record of your length costs one attention layer

Pick a record. The receipt itemizes the score matrix, its memory at half precision for eight heads, and the key-value cache that inference keeps per layer, then checks the total against a 24 GB card and an 80 GB card. Click the memory line of the receipt to swap the kernel for a tiled softmax and watch that line change. Pick the long records from your own field and see where the arithmetic breaks.

the record

Compute is T squared times d for every implementation, slide 11. Memory is T squared only when the score matrix is materialized; a tiled softmax (FlashAttention) streams it in blocks and keeps the memory linear in T, at the cost of recomputing the scores in the backward pass. The cache of slide 21 is linear in T as well, two vectors per position per layer, and it is what makes long-context generation bandwidth bound rather than compute bound: each new token reads the whole cache to do a little arithmetic.

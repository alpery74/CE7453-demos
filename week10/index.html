Message passing: depth on a graph is measured in hops

A small water network. One junction starts with a signal of 1, every other junction with 0. Each layer replaces every node's value by the normalized average of itself and its neighbours.

The layer is X_{k+1} = D^{-1/2} (A + I) D^{-1/2} X_k with the weight matrix set to the identity and no nonlinearity, which is the Kipf and Welling propagation rule on slide 7 with the learnable part removed. One hop reaches the neighbours and two hops reach their neighbours in turn. The receptive field is a graph distance.

Keep adding hops and every node drifts toward the same value: the right panel shows the spread across nodes falling toward zero. That is oversmoothing, slide 8. Two or three layers is usually what a utility network needs, and the number of layers is a statement about how far a cause is allowed to travel.

Permutation invariance: renumber the points and nothing may change

Eight scanned points. Two models read them: a plain network on the concatenated coordinates, and a shared encoder on each point followed by a max pool. Shuffle the file order and compare.

The numbers next to the points are their positions in the file. The red model multiplies the 16 concatenated coordinates by a fixed matrix, so it memorizes the order you typed the points in: shuffle and its output jumps. The blue model applies the same small encoder to every point and takes the maximum over points, and order cannot survive a max, so its output is identical after every shuffle.

Move one point and both outputs change, as they should. Order is bookkeeping and geometry is data. The model must answer to the second and ignore the first. That is the design constraint on slides 6 and 10, for point clouds and for graphs alike.

Value functions and the Bellman equation

A 6 by 6 grid where the goal pays +1 and the spillway cells pay -1. A move slips sideways with probability 0.1. Sweep the Bellman update and watch the values and the greedy arrows settle.

Each sweep applies V(s) = max over actions of the expected reward now plus gamma times V at the next state, averaged over the slip. The right panel plots the largest change per sweep. It shrinks geometrically at rate gamma, so a discount near one takes many more sweeps, and slide 15 calls the discount a policy decision for that reason.

Once the values stop moving, read the policy off them: each arrow points to the neighbour with the highest expected value. Lower the discount and the arrows near the spillway stop caring about the far-away goal.

The update is Q(s, a) += alpha (r + gamma max_a' Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)), the one line on slide 17. Its target uses the best next action, which is why the agent learns the greedy policy while behaving with exploration. The learning rate is 0.1 and the discount 0.95. Each episode starts at the bottom left and ends at a terminal cell or after 100 steps.

Run with epsilon at 0 a few times from a fresh start. The first route that happened to work gets locked in, and the greedy return stays below what the grid allows because cells off that route are never visited. Raise epsilon and the agent pays in return during training, visible in the curve, and buys a better greedy policy, visible in the readout. That is the trade on slide 17, and it is a design choice.

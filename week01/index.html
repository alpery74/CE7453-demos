Recover Ceres: fit an orbit to 24 noisy nights and say where to look in December

January 1801. Piazzi has 24 directions to a moving point of light, taken over 41 days, before the Sun swallows it. You are Gauss. The orbit is a circle round the Sun with two unknowns: its radius, which fixes the speed by Kepler's third law, and where Ceres was on 1 January. Drag the orbit to resize it and drag Ceres along it. The strip below the sky is the residual of each night. When you are satisfied, predict the direction for 31 December and see how far off you are.

The toy keeps both orbits circular and in one plane; Gauss had an ellipse in three dimensions and invented much of his method on the way. The structure is the same: a model with a few unknowns, observations with noise, a fit by least squares, and a prediction judged by whether the planet is there. The 41-day arc is short, so the sum of squares has a long valley: many radius-and-phase pairs fit January almost equally well and disagree by degrees in December. That sensitivity is why the recovery, within half a degree, made Gauss famous at 24.

Each observed difference y = h_to - h_from + e is one row of A with a +1 under the destination and a -1 under the origin; the known benchmark A has no column, so its height moves to the right-hand side. The six lines on slide 11 run unchanged: N = A'A, u = A'y, x = N^-1 u, v = y - A x, sigma0^2 = v'v / (m - n). With m = n there are no residuals and nothing can be checked; the redundancy is what buys Baarda's data snooping on slide 14.

With equal weights the light is perpendicular to the line and the shadow is the orthogonal projection, which is the picture on slide 10. With W = diag(w1, w2) the normal equations A'W(y - Ax) = 0 say the residual is W-orthogonal to the model line: w1 v1 + w2 v2 = 0. Seen in ordinary coordinates that is an oblique shadow, tilted toward the reading you trust more, and the estimate is the weighted mean (w1 y1 + w2 y2) / (w1 + w2). Gauss-Markov on slide 18 says this is the minimum-variance linear unbiased estimate exactly when the weights are the inverse variances.

Springs on the line: every observation pulls with a stiffness you set

Settlement of a footing, read eight times over twelve months. Each reading holds the fitted line with a spring whose stiffness is its weight, 1 / sigma^2. Weighted least squares minimizes the sum of w_i v_i^2, which is the elastic energy of springs with stiffness w_i stretched by v_i; the fitted line is the rest position. Slide 12's point is that the stiffnesses are a statement about the instruments, not about the data values: the same eight readings give a different rate when the tape readings are trusted as much as the gauge readings. The spec button sets the bars to the manufacturers' numbers, which is where a weight matrix comes from in practice.

The fit minimizes the sum of rho(v_i) with rho the Huber loss of slide 19, by iteratively reweighted least squares. The arrows draw psi(v) = rho'(v), the influence function of slide 20: unbounded for squared error, so the blunder pulls in proportion to how far off it is, and capped at delta beyond the knee, so the blunder can never pull harder than a point at the knee. Slide 21's three answers are the two ends and the middle of this one drag: delta large is squared error, delta small is absolute error, delta near the noise level is Huber's compromise.

The valley and the prior: shrink the circle and watch the solution walk

Two predictors of a mix's strength, cement content and total binder content, nearly the same column. The contours are the squared error over the two coefficients: a long, thin valley, so the least squares solution is sure along one direction and almost unknown along the other. The circle is a Gaussian prior on the coefficients. Drag its rim to shrink it: the solution is where the valley first touches the circle.

Minimizing |y - Aw|^2 + lambda |w|^2 is the same as least squares on A with two pseudo observations appended, each saying one coefficient is zero with weight lambda, which is the right-hand panel; it is also the maximum a posteriori estimate under a Gaussian prior w ~ N(0, sigma^2 / lambda), which is the circle. Slide 23's "normal matrix misbehaves" is the valley: N = A'A has one eigenvalue close to zero, and adding lambda to the diagonal lifts it. The grey dots are what least squares would have returned on forty other draws of the noise; the blue dots are the ridge answers. Shrinking the circle trades that scatter for a bias toward the origin.

Bet on a frame: pick the fit that will score best on data it has never seen

A bridge deck's deflection along its span, measured at twelve stations. Eight frames fit the same degree-11 polynomial to those twelve points with a penalty that grows from frame to frame. Every frame is ridge regression on a degree-11 polynomial basis with lambda from 1e-6 (frame 1) to 1e2 (frame 8), so frame 1 is interpolation through the twelve stations and frame 8 is almost a straight line. Training error falls monotonically from right to left, so it would always choose frame 1; held-out error traces the U of slide 24. Redrawing the noise shows the two terms of the bias-variance decomposition directly: the left frames change shape from draw to draw (variance), the right frames keep the same wrong shape every time (bias).

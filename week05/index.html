Place the compromise: you are the update step of a settlement monitor

A bridge pier settles about 0.2 mm per epoch and a levelling instrument reads its height each epoch. Every epoch the filter predicts, drawn as the blue bell, and the instrument reports, drawn as the orange bell. Say first whether the estimate sits closer to the sensor or to the model, then drag the marker to where you would put it; releasing the marker reveals the filter's answer and the product of the two bells. Ten epochs, then the truth is uncovered and the two tracks are scored. Change R and Q and see the computed gain move.

The update slides the answer along the line between the prediction and the measurement, and the gain says how far, slide 7: K is the predicted variance over the predicted variance plus R. That is Week 1's inverse-variance weighting, recomputed every epoch, which is why a precise instrument pulls the answer toward itself and a confident prediction holds it back. Predict grows the variance by Q, update shrinks it, and the posterior bell is narrower than either input, slide 6. Your implied gain is read off where you dropped the marker; after a few epochs most people settle near the computed one without being told it, because the two bells show the right answer.

Cut the cable: paint an outage on a landslide record and watch the band breathe

A GNSS station on a slow landslide reports displacement every day for 200 days, and a constant-velocity filter tracks it with a 95 percent band. A safety rule requires the band to stay narrower than 40 mm. Write down how many days the receiver can be down before the rule breaks, then drag across the timeline to black out an outage; the filter keeps predicting through it and admits its growing ignorance, and the computed limit appears after the first cut. Through the outage only the predict step runs, so the covariance grows by F P F transposed plus Q at every epoch with nothing to shrink it, slide 8; for a constant-velocity model the position variance grows with the cube of the outage length, because an uncertain velocity integrates. On reacquisition the predicted variance dwarfs R, the gain goes to nearly one, and the first measurement snaps the band shut, slide 9. How long an outage can last is set by Q: a smaller Q is a stronger claim that the slide moves steadily, and it buys a longer outage at the price of a slower response if the slope accelerates.

Optimally wrong: a linear filter on a nonlinear catchment, and the knob that cannot fix it

Rain falls on a catchment whose discharge responds nonlinearly: big storms drain faster than small ones. Someone wrote the filter for this record with a linear reservoir, and at the default Q the big peaks come out low. Commit to whether raising Q will remove that bias, then turn the knob and try. Below the hydrograph, a stem plot shows the innovations, the surprises; their autocorrelation is the diagnostic that says whether the model is wrong. The next tab hands the filter the true dynamics.

The filter is optimal under the model it was given, and the model is wrong, slide 12: the linear reservoir drains large storms too slowly and small ones too quickly, so the big peaks come out low and the small one high whatever Q is. Raising Q only tells the filter to trust the measurements more, and it chases their noise while keeping the bias. Innovations expose it: for a correct model they are white, uncorrelated from one day to the next, and a lag-one autocorrelation outside the band is the signature of a transition that does not describe the system, slide 10. Fixing the transition is the subject of the next tab.

The extended filter: hand the filter the true dynamics and linearize at every step

Same catchment, same gauge. This filter carries the true transition and, on each day, uses its slope at the current estimate in place of the linear reservoir's fixed coefficient. Commit to whether its innovations will be white, then run it. The right-hand panel sets the numeric Jacobian d f / d S at the day you click next to the linear model's 0.85, and the readout puts the two filters side by side. Given the true dynamics, the extended filter of slide 11 linearizes them at each estimate: the Jacobian d f / d S, taken numerically at the current state, stands in for F, and the rest of the loop is unchanged. Here the carried state S is the discharge. The slope of the true transition sits near 0.9 at base flow, jumps above one on a storm day, when added water raises tomorrow's discharge more than one for one, and falls to about a third on the day after a peak, when a full store drains fast; a fixed 0.85 expresses none of this. With the right transition the peak bias and the autocorrelation both go, and the honest Q is small because the catchment itself is nearly deterministic. Linearizing fails when the curvature over one step is large or the current estimate is already poor; the unscented filter pushes sigma points through the true map instead.

Slide 14 keeps the skeleton exactly: carry a state, fold in the arrival, emit an estimate, repeat. F becomes the recurrent weights, H the output weights, and the measurement is now called the input, slide 15. Most people miss the gain: in the filter it is recomputed every epoch from P and R; in the cell it is baked into the input weights once, the same for every arrival, which is why a plain cell cannot weight a bad reading down. P is dropped outright, so there is no band, and Q and R are never written because the training record and the loss decide what the cell trusts.

The chain of factors: how far back can the training signal reach?

A recurrent cell unrolled over forty daily readings, the loss at the last step. Travelling back, the gradient is multiplied by one cell Jacobian per step; here each factor is the recurrent weight times the slope of the activation. Each cell is shaded by how much gradient reaches it, and the reach marker shows the earliest cell that still receives one percent. Write down how far the gradient reaches at w = 1.05 before the slider opens, then set the recurrent weight and watch the knife edge at one on a linear cell, and the double loss on a tanh cell, which saturates above one; then open the gated memory line, which replaces the chain of products with a chain of gate values.

Unrolling turns the loop into a forty-layer network with tied weights, slide 16, and backpropagation through time is the chain rule along it, slide 17. What sets the growth rate of the product is the largest factor: below one the signal decays geometrically and the early days receive nothing usable, above one it explodes, and one exactly is a knife edge that no plain cell sits on for long, slide 18. Clipping bounds the explosion and leaves the decay untouched, which is why vanishing needed an architectural fix. On the gated line the factor at each step is the forget gate itself, and a gate near one carries the signal back intact, slide 19.

Valves on a memory line: set a gate policy that remembers a flood for nine days assignment

Four twelve-day gauge records: in two of them a flood reading arrives early, in two it never comes. At day twelve the cell must say whether a flood happened. Picture the memory line as a pipe with three valves on it. Write the policy with the three controls: how much the forget valve keeps each day, the reading above which the input valve opens, and the day from which the output valve reveals the memory. Record 1 is the default; the other three and the all-records view are the assignment. One policy runs on every record shown and the score is the worst error among them, with a plain cell below for comparison. The memory line is additive, slide 19: the forget valve scales what is already there, the input valve adds a fresh candidate, and nothing on the way passes through a matrix, so a forget value of one holds the flood reading unchanged for nine days and holds the gradient the same way. An input valve that opens only for large readings is a sigmoid of the input with its own weights, a learned policy; here you write it by hand. Below it, the plain cell has no valve: every day it pushes the whole state through the recurrent weight and the activation, and the flood fades. Tying the valves so that what enters is one minus what is kept is the GRU's update gate, slide 20, which leaves one control where there were two.

The telegram: compress a discharge record into three numbers, then see what the decoder gets back

An encoder must summarize a daily discharge record into a context vector of fixed size, three numbers, and a decoder must rebuild the record from those three alone. Decoding is a linear reconstruction fitted on three hundred records; a learned three-number code, the best any linear encoder can do, is drawn alongside. Commit first to the record length at which the telegram's error passes one half; the error curve then opens, and you can change the three summaries and the record length and watch what a fixed budget does. Everything the decoder will ever know has to pass through the context vector, and its size does not grow with the record, slide 23: ten days and two hundred days are squeezed into the same three numbers, so the error climbs with length for a structural reason, and the learned code climbs with it. Errors are reported as a fraction of the spread across records, so one means no better than the overall mean. Slide 22 is the lower panel: fed the true previous value at every step, teacher forcing, the one-step decoder tracks; fed its own output, it drifts, because one early error becomes the next step's input. Attention, next week, lets the decoder reread every encoder step, with no summary in between.
